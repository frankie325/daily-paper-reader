# Abstract 

Real-time object detectors achieve remarkable accuracy under controlled condi-tions, yet degrade sharply on non-ideal inputs: fisheye distortion, game-rendered characters, aerial viewpoints, and 360 ◦ panoramas. We present YOLOv14 , a detec-tion framework with four adaptive mechanisms designed for specific types of input variation: (1) Deformable Area-Attention with windowed computation and shifted windows for geometric distortion; (2) Multi-level Game2Real Alignment with pro-gressive adversarial training for domain shift; (3) View-Aware Contrastive Learning with adaptive temperature for viewpoint invariance; and (4) Scene-Adaptive Aug-mentation with dynamic loss balancing for scene diversity. Together, YOLOv14 

achieves 49.1 mAP on COCO val2017 at 2.91 ms (T4 GPU), and delivers substan-tial gains on fisheye (+4.1 mAP), panorama (+6.6 mAP), drone (+6.4 mAP), and our synthesized game-character benchmark (+26.1 mAP). We release code and models to facilitate reproducible research. 

# 1 Introduction 

The YOLO family [1–3] has driven real-time object detection from 15 to over 300 frames per second while steadily improving accuracy. However, all existing detectors share a critical unspoken assumption: the input image is captured by an ideal pinhole camera under standard conditions. This assumption breaks in four practical scenarios: • Geometric distortion. Fisheye and wide-angle lenses introduce radial barrel distortion. • Cross-domain rendering. Game engines produce distinct visual properties. • Extreme viewpoints. Drone and ground-level views present objects at unfamiliar angles. • 360 ◦ panoramas. Equirectangular projections cause latitude-dependent stretching. We hypothesize that these challenges can be effectively addressed by input-adaptive feature sampling and normalization. Based on this hypothesis, we propose YOLOv14 , a framework with four complementary adaptations. Our contributions are: 1. Deformable Area-Attention with shifted windows for geometric robustness (§2.2). 2. Multi-level Game2Real Alignment with progressive training (§2.3). 3. View-Aware Contrastive Learning with adaptive temperature (§2.4). 4. Scene-Adaptive Augmentation with dynamic loss balancing (§2.5). 5. A multi-domain evaluation suite with detailed protocols (§3.1).

# 2 Related Work 

Real-time object detection. The YOLO lineage [1, 2] has evolved through successive architectural innovations: YOLOv3 [4] introduced multi-scale prediction; YOLOv4 [5] systematized bag-of-freebies; YOLOX [6] advanced anchor-free design; YOLOv6 [7] focused on hardware-aware re-parameterization; YOLOv7 [8] introduced trainable bag-of-freebies; PP-YOLOE [9] demonstrated anchor-free efficiency; Gold-YOLO [10] proposed gather-and-distribute fusion; YOLOv12 [3] introduced area-attention. Beyond YOLO, FCOS [13], EfficientDet [14], DETR [15], and DINO [16] have pushed the state-of-the-art. Our work builds on YOLOv12 but extends it to cross-domain non-ideal inputs. 

Deformable convolutions and attention. DCNv1/v2 [20, 21] learn per-kernel offsets. Deformable DETR [19] introduced sparse deformable attention. Our D-AAttn generalizes deformable sampling to dense area-attention with windowed computation. Related works include deformable Vision Transformers [22] and CSWin Transformer [23]. 

Domain adaptation for detection. Prior methods include DA-Faster R-CNN [24], DANN [25], MCD [28], SWDA [26], and conditional adversarial adaptation [29]. CycleGAN [27] and Deep-JDOT [30] further advanced unsupervised adaptation. Our multi-level alignment integrates data, feature, and adversarial techniques. 

Multi-view and panoramic detection. Aerial detection [32] and panoramic methods [33] use task-specific designs. WoodScape [34] and OmniDet [35] address fisheye and surround-view. Our method handles diverse inputs through adaptive conditioning modules. 

Data augmentation. AutoAugment [36], RandAugment [37], and TrivialAugment [38] learn optimal policies. MixUp [40] and CutMix [41] regularize training. Our AdaptiveAugmentPolicy routes inputs to scene-specific augmentations. 

Loss functions. GIoU [43], DFL [44], DIoU/CIoU [45], SIoU [46], OTA [47], and VarifocalNet [48] have improved bounding-box regression and label assignment. 

Domain adaptation theory. The H∆H-divergence framework of Ben-David et al. [49] provides theoretical grounding. Our design is empirically guided by these principles.

3.7 Training Objective 

The total loss combines detection, offset regularization, domain adversarial, and cross-view losses: 

Ltotal = Ldet + λreg Loffset + λ1Ladv + λ2Lcross , (12) with λreg = 0 .01 , λ1 = 0 .1 (annealed), λ2 = 0 .05 .

3.8 Design Hypothesis 

Our design is guided by the following hypotheses, validated through ablations: 

Hypothesis 1: Dense deformation improves coverage. Windowed D-AAttn with shifted windows balances adaptability and efficiency. Ablations show D-AAttn outperforms standard area-attention on fisheye by +3.6 mAP. 

Hypothesis 2: Multi-level alignment reduces domain divergence. Data-level stylization, AdaIN, and adversarial training progressively reduce the domain gap. Progressive training prevents catas-trophic forgetting. 

Hypothesis 3: Adaptive components are complementary. Each component targets a different variation source; their combination yields super-additive gains. 

3.9 Multi-Domain Benchmark Construction 

We construct four benchmarks for systematic evaluation. 

3.9.1 COCO-Fisheye 

We apply barrel distortion k ∈ { 0.10 , 0.15 , 0.20 } to COCO val2017. The transformation is rout =

rin (1 + k∥rin ∥2). Bounding boxes are warped by transforming the four corners and taking the axis-aligned rectangle; boxes with corners outside the crop are discarded. 

3.9.2 COCO-Panorama (Primary Panorama Benchmark) 

We generate equirectangular panoramas from COCO val2017 images using spherical projection. Each COCO image is mapped to a 2:1 equirectangular projection (width = 2 × height, with height fixed to 640 pixels for consistency). The transformation is: 

θ = 2 πx/W, ϕ = πy/H, (13) 5where (x, y ) are pixel coordinates, (θ, ϕ ) are spherical coordinates. Bounding boxes are transformed by projecting each corner to spherical coordinates and then to equirectangular coordinates. We generate 5,000 images. The validation set uses the same split as COCO val2017 to avoid overlap. Horizontal boundary continuity is maintained by wrapping pixels at x = 0 and x = W .

3.9.3 VisDrone (Drone) 

We use the standard VisDrone-DET 2021 validation set (5,480 images, 10 classes). 

3.9.4 Game-Stylized COCO (Delta-Syn) 

We apply global game-style rendering to COCO val2017 images. This is a controlled stress test for domain adaptation. We report the domain gap under standard augmentations (24.1 mAP) and our adaptation (50.2 mAP).

4.1 Implementation Details 

We train on COCO train2017 for 300 epochs using SGD (momentum 0.937, weight decay 5 × 10 −4), cosine LR (initial 1 × 10 −2), batch size 256 on 4 A100 GPUs, input size 640. Inference: T4 GPU with TensorRT FP16 (batch size 1). mAP averaged over three independent runs with random seeds. We report seeds and individual results in Appendix.

Both use the same backbone and training schedule as our method, but without the Game2Real data-level stylization (to keep them as pure UDA methods). # 5 Conclusion 

We presented YOLOv14 , an adaptive detection framework for diverse imaging conditions. Four components address specific types of input variation: Deformable Area-Attention (geometric), Game2Real Alignment (domain), View-Aware Learning (viewpoint), and Scene-Adaptive Routing (scene). While the components are specialized, their combination yields state-of-the-art performance across standard and challenging benchmarks. 

Limitations: The Game benchmark is synthetic; real-game performance is lower. The SUN360 dataset is a heuristic proxy. The D-AAttn windowed attention has higher FLOPs than standard convolution. A.1 Network Architecture Details Deformable Offset Network: 

• Input: feature map X ∈ RB×C×H×W

• Conv2d( C, C/ 4, 3, 1, 1) + BN + SiLU • Conv2d( C/ 4, C/ 4, 3, 1, 1) + BN + SiLU • Conv2d( C/ 4, 2, 3, 1, 1, bias=True) 11 • Weights initialized to zero for identity mapping. 

DomainAdaptiveLayer: Inserted after the P2 (1/4 scale, 128 channels) and P4 (1/16 scale, 512 chan-nels) outputs of the backbone. Architecture: AdaptiveAvgPool2d(1) → Linear( C, max(16, C/ 16 )) →

ReLU → Linear(max(16, C/ 16 ), 2). The output logit for “game” is used as pgame after sigmoid. 

CircularConv: Applied to all convolutional layers from the first downsampling layer onward (stride 2 layers and their subsequent stride 1 layers in the later stages). For stride-1 convolutions in early stages, standard zero-padding is used to avoid over-regularization. 

SphereAAttn: NL = 4 bands. The first and last bands (polar) use 4 heads; the two equatorial bands use 8 heads. This allocation is fixed based on validation performance. 

A.2 Temperature Decay and Hyperparameter Sensitivity 

The adaptive temperature τ (t) = 0 .05 + 0 .45 e−0.01 t decays exponentially. We tested linear decay and step decay; exponential gave the best validation performance. β = 0 .01 was selected from a coarse grid search over {0.005 , 0.01 , 0.02 }.

A.3 COCO-Panorama Generation Protocol 

We generate COCO-Panorama as follows: 1. Input: COCO val2017 images (5,000 images, 640 × 640 pixels). 2. Spherical projection: For each pixel (x, y ) in the output equirectangular image ( 1280 × 640 ), compute spherical coordinates (θ, ϕ ):

θ = 2 πx/ 1280 , ϕ = πy/ 640 − π/ 2. (14) 3. Sampling: For each output pixel, sample the input image at coordinates (θ, ϕ ) using bilinear interpolation. Boundary wrapping: x = 0 wraps to x = 1280 ; y = 0 maps to the pole (sampled from the top row). 4. Bounding box transformation: Each box corner is projected to spherical coordinates and then to equirectangular coordinates. The new box is the axis-aligned rectangle of the transformed corners. 5. Discard boxes that fall outside the valid region after transformation. The validation split uses the original COCO val2017 indices to avoid overlap.
