## Abstract 

Camera-only multi-view 3D object detection in autonomous driving has witnessed encouraging developments in recent years, largely attributed to the revolution of fundamental ar-chitectures in modeling bird’s eye view (BEV). Despite the growing overall average performance, we contend that the exploration of more specific and challenging corner cases hasn’t received adequate attention. In this work, we delve into a specific yet critical issue for safe autonomous driv-ing: occlusion. To alleviate this challenge, we draw inspi-ration from the human amodal perception system, which is proven to have the capacity for mentally reconstructing the complete semantic concept of occluded objects with prior knowledge. More specifically, we introduce auxiliary visual and language prototypes, akin to human prior knowledge, to enhance the diminished object features caused by oc-clusion. Inspired by Siamese object tracking, we fuse the information from these prototypes with the baseline model through an efficient depth-wise correlation, thereby enhanc-ing the quality of object-related features and guiding the learning of 3D object queries, especially for partially oc-cluded ones. Furthermore, we propose the random pixel drop to mimic occlusion and the multi-modal contrastive loss to align visual features of different occlusion levels to a unified space during training. Our inspiration originates from addressing occlusion, however, we are surprised to find that the proposed framework also enhances robustness in various challenging scenarios that diminish object repre-sentation, such as inclement weather conditions. By apply-ing our model to different baselines, i.e., BEVFormer and SparseBEV, we demonstrate consistent improvements.

For instance, the failure to detect a partially occluded pedestrian near the ego car has minimal impact on average performance but is crucial for ensuring safe autonomous driving. Recent works like RoboBEV [48] have reassessed the limitation of the current evaluation pro-tocol and advocated for assessing methods under different scenarios of interest. Besides, methods designed for speci-fied cases like long-range detection [15] are also introduced to address corner cases. While encouraged by the notable progress, we notice that the occlusion problem remains less explored. This motivates us to fill the gap in this work. It’s easy to understand that there is no chance to local-ize the completely occluded target with solely single-frame knowledge. One effective approach that has been demon-strated to alleviate this challenge is the incorporation of more temporal information. Yet this is not the topic of our work. We primarily tend to enhance the detection quality of partially occluded objects with only single-frame clues. By doing so, our proposed method can seamlessly integrate with almost all existing methods regardless of their baseline architecture design. The resilience of our method will be proved in the later experiments. Then, what is the inherent difficulty in detecting partially occluded objects? Gener-ally, occlusion diminishes the quality of learned visual rep-resentation as fewer discernible features are available com-pared with non-occluded objects. It’s akin to a chicken-and-egg scenario because detecting occluded objects neces-sitates sufficient contextual cues, yet such information is in-herently limited due to occlusion. So, could we introduce additional clues for occluded objects? 

We try to find the answer from the human vision and recognition systems, which are proven to have the capacity for mentally reconstructing the complete semantic concept of occluded objects with prior knowledge [17, 19]. This ability, known as amodal perception, enables us to assimi-late integral information from both visible cues and imper-ceptible signals [36]. Inspired by this mechanism, we pro-pose a novel plug-and-play architecture termed CorrBEV, which incorporates auxiliary semantic prototypes for occlu-sion reasoning. As an old saying goes, “A picture paints a thousand words” . One intuitive way is to supply richer vi-sual priors from image templates that correspond with dif-ferent human-defined object categories. Besides, human language, specifically the category name itself, encapsu-lates compact knowledge about the described object, which has already demonstrated effectiveness in improving vision tasks [9, 10, 29, 37, 52]. Therefore, it sounds reasonable to introduce language clues alongside the visual priors to en-hance representation learning of partially occluded objects. We provide an example to illustrate this motivation in Fig. 1. More specifically, our proposed framework consists of three core components. Firstly, the Multi-modal Proto-type Generator provides prior knowledge for improving the representation quality of targets. We crop images with 2D ground-truth boxes in the training dataset and use a pub-licly available model to extract feature embeddings. The feature embeddings are then clustered based on visibility level 1 and category to form occlusion-aware visual proto-types. The language prototypes are obtained by transform-ing category names ( e.g. pedestrian) to embeddings with pre-trained BERT [8]. Secondly, the Correlation-guided Query Learner injects the knowledge of multi-modal pro-totypes to the model, which is inspired by Siamese Object Tracking [1, 20, 55]. The depth-wise correlation operator transforms prototypes and backbone features into salient maps, which we find partially occluded objects can be bet-ter localized than that in 3D space. The decoder then gen-erates 3D object queries with the salient maps and sam-ples 2D features from both the backbone and the corre-lation feature in a dual way. Last but not least, during training, the pseudo-occlusion processor in our proposed 

Occlusion-aware Trainer randomly drops pixels in less-occluded objects, which can balance the distribution of ob-jects with different occlusion levels. Besides, we also pro-pose to align vision and language representation with con-trastive loss [32]. Considering one language embedding as a cluster center, the alignment training forces the model to predict closer representation in the latent space for occluded and non-occluded objects of the same category. To demonstrate the effectiveness and generality of the proposed method, we apply our model to both BEV-Former [25] (dense-query) and SparseBEV [28] (sparse-query). Without bells and whistles, our model improves the aforementioned two baselines for 2.6%/2.7% mAP and 2.6%/1.6% NDS on nuScenes [2] validation split, respec-tively. The advances are maintained in our introduced occlusion-aware evaluation. More surprisingly, while our approach mainly aims at mitigating the negative effects of occlusion, we observed that our method also enhances the robustness of baseline techniques in other challenging sce-narios from RoboBEV [48]. The results highlight the re-silience of our proposal, which can effectively address the diminished representation caused by various conditions en-countered in the real world. In summary, our contributions are four-fold: ( i) We pro-pose a plug-and-play model that incorporates auxiliary vi-sual and language clues to improve the robustness of de-tection methods, especially on partially occluded objects. 

(ii) We innovatively introduce correlation learning to ele-gantly inject the knowledge of multi-modal prototypes into multi-view 3D detection models. (iii) We optimize the train-ing pipeline of multi-view detection methods by proposing

## 2. Related Work 

Multi-view Camera-based 3D Object Detection. Bene-fiting from the intrinsic complementarity in different obser-vation angles, recent year’s attention in camera-based 3D object detection is turning from a single-view to its multi-view counterparts. The related methods can be roughly categorized into two branches. The first one explicitly constructs dense BEV representations by lifting the 2D image features to 3D space. Specifically, the pioneering work LSS [35] performs view transformation with the pre-dicted categorical depth distribution of each pixel. The follow-up method BEVDet [14] designs rich image-level and BEV-level augmentations in training. BEVDepth [24] and BEVStereo [23] further improve the quality of BEV features with explicit depth supervision. Another branch densely or sparsely projects 3D queries to 2D images for sampling and aggregating visual information. The pioneer-ing work DETR3D [44] is inspired by its 2D parent DETR [4], which builds relations between sparse 3D object queries with 2D points in the images. BEVFormer [25] chooses to explicitly learn BEV representation. PETR [30] employs 3D position coding to generate 3D position-aware features. Sparse4D [26] and SparseBEV [28] can be regarded as the temporal version of DETR3D [44], which explores a fully sparse manner to aggregate multi-frame features. As the recent consensus in practical application sug-gests, handling corner cases may be the last mile of per-ception systems. The research community also proposes different works to catch up with this trend. For instance, RoboBEV[48] tags the nuScenes with different challenging attributes for more sufficiently evaluating models. Far3D [15] and LR3D [50] pay attention to long-distance objects, which tends to expand the spatial range of BEV detection methods. OpenSight [53] proposes to leverage multi-modal LiDAR and camera data for open-vocabulary 3D object de-tection. In this work, we focus on handling occlusion cases which are essential for safe autonomous driving. 

Handling Occlusion in 3D Object Detection. Occlu-sion presents an inevitable and critical challenge in au-tonomous driving, particularly in the realm of 3D ob-ject detection. Significant efforts have been made to ad-dress this issue through LiDAR-based [12, 40, 49] and camera-based [5, 18, 39, 51] solutions. In LiDAR-based approaches, a common strategy involves recovering hid-den 3D information. For example, WYSIWYG [12] min-imizes shape distortion due to occlusion by utilizing visi-bility information via a raycasting algorithm. BtcDet [49] incorporates a sub-network designed to estimate the com-plete occupancy of objects in occluded regions. Camera-based methods tackle occlusion through modeling 2D oc-cluded keypoints [39], reconstructing boxes using shape priors [18], predicting 2D depth maps [41], and estimating uncertainty [5]. Additionally, a widely recognized strategy for mitigating the adverse effects of occlusion involves the fusion of temporal information [26, 31, 33, 42]. However, temporal models may lose efficacy for moving objects due to the challenges of accurate motion compensation. More-over, the ambiguous context of occluded targets and their foreground objects can diminish the quality of temporal learning. In camera-based multi-view 3D object detection, we observed that the occlusion challenge has received lim-ited attention. Previous work OccluBEV [46] predicts 3D voxel segmentation masks based on labels derived from Li-DAR points. Differently, we propose a general framework that leverages vision-language multi-modal information to enhance diminished object representations caused by occlu-sion and other challenging driving environments, without relying on additional LiDAR data. 

## 3. Method 

This section details our proposed framework CorrBEV, as shown in Fig. 2. We first delineate the process of gener-ating visual and language prototypes in Sec. 3.1. Then, in Sec. 3.2, we propose correlation learning to use the prior knowledge of multi-modal prototypes. Finally, Sec. 3.3 in-troduces the designed occlusion-aware trainer. 

3.1. Multi-modal Prototype Generator 

As aforementioned, insufficient contextual cues introduce an inherent challenge in detecting occluded objects. In this work, we propose the Multi-modal Prototype Generator to mitigate this problem by imitating human imagination abil-ity with auxiliary prior knowledge beyond the observations (images). Specifically, for learning visual prototypes, we offline crop 2D object templates in the bounding boxes and use DeViT [54] to transform these templates to feature em-beddings Pv ∈ RK×N ×D , where K and N denote the number of samples and categories, respectively. D is the number of channels. As we primarily want to alleviate the occlusion problem, we split the prototypes based on the vis-ibility label of each ground-truth box and then average the 

K samples, obtaining Pvo ∈ R(N ×M )×D . M is the number of visibility levels ( N = 10 , M = 4 in nuScenes [2]). For the language prototypes, we use the pre-trained BERT [8] to extract high-level semantic information in object category names, obtaining Pl ∈ RN ×D . To adapt the prototypes to the detection task, we fine-tune the text encoder during training. Since we use the relatively light BERT encoder, rare training costs would be introduced (see experiments). The visual prototypes are offline generated, therefore the image encoder is frozen in training. 3.2. Correlation-guided Query Learner 

The next step is to fuse the multi-modal prototypes with baseline models. There are three predefined goals: 1) guar-antee the efficiency; 2) enhance the feature quality, espe-cially the partially occluded objects; 3) guide the detection of partially occluded objects. In this work, we introduce the correlation learner to satisfy all the requirements. For efficiency, we introduce the depth-wise correlation from Siamese Object Tracking [1, 56] to fuse multi-modal prototypes with the backbone features Fb ∈ RH×W ×D . H

3.3. Occlusion-aware Trainer 

Pseudo-occlusion Processor. As shown in Fig. 3, the num-ber of objects with different visibility levels is obviously unequal, which poses a challenge to optimize targets in mi-norities. Motivated by data augmentation in 2D tasks [57], we introduce a pseudo-occlusion processor ( P2 ) to balance the distribution of objects with different visibility levels. Specifically, the proposed P2 randomly drops pixels in the 2D bounding boxes of non-occluded objects ( i.e., with vis-ibility value of 3 or 4 in nuScenes [2]) and replaces the dropped pixels with mean values of the region. 

Contrastive Semantic Alignment. Contrastive learning was generally used in self-supervised learning [6] and multi-modal alignment [38]. We introduce contrastive learning in this work to: 1) intra-class: align the representa-tion of objects with different occlusion levels to one unified space ( i.e. , language), therefore enhancing the classification accuracy of partially occluded objects, 2) inter-class: en-large the representation distinguishability of different cate-gories. In particular, we follow previous works [21, 29] ap-plying dot product between each query with language proto-types to predict logits and compute focal loss for each query. 

## 4. Experiments 

4.1. Datasets and Metrics 

Following our baselines [25, 28], we conduct experiments on the nuScenes [2] dataset. The benchmark comprises 1000 driving scenes and is split into 700/150/150 for train-ing/validation/testing. For 3D object detection, there are about 1.4M annotated 3D bounding boxes from 10 classes. Alongside coordinates and scale labels of the 3D bounding box, the dataset also provides object visibility levels. The visibility levels are officially divided into four ranges, i.e., 

0-40%, 40-60%, 60-80%, and 80-100%. Since occlusion is the key factor for low-visibility samples, we regard the performances on four visibility levels as the indicator of de-tection robustness under different occlusion conditions. For evaluating and comparing the detection perfor-mances of different methods, we adopt the mean average precision (mAP) and nuScenes detection score (NDS) as primary metrics, as well as five True Positive (TP) metrics, including mean Average Translation Error (mATE), mean Average Scale Error (mASE), mean Average Orientation Error (mAOE), mean Average Velocity Error (mAVE), and mean Average Attribute Error (mAAE). Additionally, we report the recall of four visibility levels to evaluate the de-tection capability on partially occluded objects. 

4.2. Implementation Details 

As mentioned before, the proposed method can be easily plugged into recent multi-view 3D object detection meth-ods. To prove the generality of our method, we apply it to both sparse-query based SparseBEV [28] and dense-query based BEVFormer [25]. For fair comparisons, we adopt the same training settings with the baselines. All experiments are performed with 8 NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPUs.

4.4. Component-wise Analysis 

Different Prototype Settings. In our framework, the in-troduced multi-modal prototypes provide prior knowledge to compensate the diminished visual features through query initialization and sampling. As shown in Tab. 3, we study the influence of different settings for the prototypes. ① de-notes the baseline results (SparseBEV [28]). By exploiting the text prototype for occlusion-aware correlation learning, the performance noticeably improves for 1.1% mAP and 1.5% NDS respectively ( ②v.s. ①). Applying both text and visual prototypes further brings gains for 0.6% mAP and 0.3% NDS ( ③v.s. ②). This demonstrates the effectiveness of our design for 3D detection, as well as the complementar-ity of visual and language knowledge. Notably, the perfor-mance of each visibility level obtains consistent improve-ments, showing the enhanced capability of our method in detecting objects in different occlusion conditions. 

Different Strategies in Occlusion-aware Training. We improve the training strategies of baseline framework by introducing the pseudo-occlusion processor (P2) and multi-modal contrastive learning (CL). Tab. 3 shows that CL brings gains of 0.4% mAP and 0.5% NDS ( ④vs. ③). Ap-plying P2 further improves for 0.6% mAP and 0.3% NDS (⑤vs. ④). The performance of each visibility level is also consistently enhanced. Notably, the proposed two training strategies introduce rare training costs and zero inference burden. Meanwhile surprisingly, changing the occlusion distribution of training samples doesn’t bring negative ef-fects to the overall performances, which we believe more methods can benefit from this cost-free lunch.

## 5. Conclusion 

In this paper, we propose CorrBEV, a plug-and-play frame-work to address the occlusion challenge for camera-based multi-view 3D detection task. The essence is introducing multi-modal prototypes as prior knowledge for occlusion reasoning. By incorporating the well-designed occlusion-aware trainer, we demonstrate consistent improvements in two different baseline frameworks. Besides, we conduct a thorough evaluation and analysis of detection performance under different occlusion conditions, which we believe is an insightful contribution to the field.
